Role-based modules

Scenario modules for the situations freelancers, virtual assistants, and contractors actually meet in client work. These build on the five attack types. Read those first if you have not.

Premium 12 min read Four role-based scenarios

Client Impersonation

Someone claiming to be your client, or someone new at your client's company

For a freelancer or virtual assistant, the client relationship is the attack surface. You take instructions from people you have never met, over channels you did not choose, often across time zones that make a quick check awkward. An attacker who can convincingly wear your client's identity does not need to break anything technical.

The three shapes it takes

  • The new contact. A person introduces themselves as recently joined at your client, a new project manager, a new finance lead. Plausible, unverifiable, and the reason nobody recognises them.
  • The compromised mailbox. Your real client's account has been taken over, so the address is genuine and the thread history is real. This is the hardest version to spot.
  • The lookalike domain. One character different from the real company domain, with a signature block copied from a genuine email.

What they usually want

Access first, money second. Shared logins to the client's social accounts, ad platforms, or CRM. Admin rights on a tool you manage. A copy of the customer list. Only later does a payment request appear, because by then the relationship feels established.

The verification rule that works

Confirm every new contact through the person who introduced you to the account. Not by replying to the new contact's message, and not by calling a number in their signature, through your existing relationship. One message to your known contact: "Is Ramon in finance yours? He has asked me for portal access." That is the entire defence, and it costs a minute.

Standing arrangement worth proposing. Ask each client, at the start of a contract, who is authorised to request access or payment changes, and agree that anyone new gets introduced by them first. Clients tend to appreciate being asked. It also puts the arrangement in writing before you need it.

Invoice and Payment Scams

The attack that costs freelancers the most money, and the single rule that stops it

Invoice fraud is where social engineering turns directly into lost income. There are two directions, and freelancers are exposed to both.

Direction one: your invoice is intercepted

An attacker inside your mailbox, or your client's, watches until an invoice goes out. They then send a follow-up: "apologies, please disregard the account details on the invoice, we have changed banks", and your client pays them instead of you. You find out weeks later when you chase a late payment.

Direction two: you are asked to pay

A supplier, subcontractor, or tool you use emails to say their details have changed. Or a "client" overpays you and asks for the difference back to a different account. The original payment then reverses as fraudulent and you are out the refund.

The overpayment variant

Treat any overpayment followed by a refund request as fraud until proven otherwise. The incoming payment is made with a stolen card or a bad cheque; the refund you send is your own money, and it is gone. Ask them to reverse the original transaction through their bank instead of refunding it yourself.

The rule

Any change to where money goes is confirmed by voice, on a number you already had, before anything is paid. No exceptions for people you know well. Those are exactly the accounts worth compromising.

Two habits that make you a harder target

  • State on every invoice: "Our bank details never change. Any email claiming otherwise is fraudulent. Please call to verify." It protects your clients and your own payments at once.
  • Send invoices as PDFs from an address with multi-factor authentication enabled, and check your sent folder and mail rules periodically for anything you did not create.

Fake Job and Recruiter Offers

Why remote job seekers are targeted, and what separates a real offer from a harvest

Job scams work because they arrive when your guard is lowest, when you want the outcome to be real. For remote workers between contracts, an unsolicited approach with good money attached is exactly the message least likely to be scrutinised.

Four patterns

  • Credential harvest. A "skills assessment" or "candidate portal" that asks you to sign in with Google or Microsoft. The assessment does not exist; the login page is the whole point.
  • Advance fee. Training materials, certification, equipment, or a background check that you must pay for. Legitimate employers never charge you to be hired.
  • Money mule recruitment. A "payment processing" or "financial operations" role where you receive funds and forward them onward, keeping a percentage. This is laundering other people's stolen money, and the legal exposure lands on you.
  • Identity harvest. Onboarding paperwork requesting your government ID, bank details, and tax number before any contract exists.

What a real process looks like

A real employer has a website with a careers page that lists the role. The recruiter's email uses the company's own domain. There is a conversation before an offer, usually a call. Documents like ID and bank details are collected after a signed contract, through an HR system, not through chat. And nobody skips steps because they were impressed by your portfolio.

Checks worth two minutes

  • Search the company name plus the word "scam" before replying.
  • Find the company's real website yourself and check whether the role is listed there.
  • Look at the recruiter's profile age, connection count, and activity history.
  • If they move you to Telegram or WhatsApp immediately, stop. Off-platform means unmonitored and unreportable.
The tell that never fails: a real job costs you nothing and asks for your credentials never. Any offer that inverts either of those is not an offer.

Secure Client Data Handling

You hold more than your job title suggests. Here is how to hold it responsibly

Freelancers and virtual assistants routinely hold access that would require a formal approval process inside a company: client inboxes, customer databases, ad accounts, financial records, admin credentials. You get that access because you need it to work, and usually with no security onboarding attached.

Collect less

The safest data is the data you never held. Ask whether you actually need the full export or just the fields you work with. Ask whether you need ongoing access or access for one task. Anything you do not have cannot leak from you.

Ask for delegated access, not shared logins

Being added as a user on a client's account is safer for both sides than being handed the owner's password. It gives you your own credentials, it gives them an audit trail, and it lets them remove your access cleanly when the contract ends. Most platforms support it. Where a client insists on sharing a password, use a password manager's sharing feature rather than chat or email.

Keep it in their systems

Work in the client's Drive, their project tool, their CRM. Copying data into your personal accounts multiplies the number of places it can leak from and blurs who is responsible for it.

Delete at the end

When a project closes, remove client files from your machine and your cloud storage unless you have been asked to retain them. Old archives are pure liability: no upside, permanent exposure.

If something goes wrong

  • Tell the client the same day. Fast disclosure is forgiven; late discovery is not.
  • Write down what was accessed, when, and by what route, while it is fresh.
  • Revoke the access involved and rotate anything that was shared.
  • In the Philippines, if personal data belonging to others was exposed, the client may have obligations to the National Privacy Commission, which is another reason they need to hear it from you immediately.
Worth putting in your contract. A short clause on how client data is stored, how long you keep it, and what happens at the end of the engagement. It sets expectations and it makes you look like a professional who has thought about this, because you have.
