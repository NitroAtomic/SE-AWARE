Fake Job and Recruiter Offers

Approaches engineered to arrive when you are between contracts and least likely to scrutinise them.

Premium 7 min read 10-question quiz at the end

What it is

Job scams work because they arrive when your guard is at its lowest, which is when you want the outcome to be real. For a freelancer between contracts, an unsolicited approach with good money attached is the single message least likely to be scrutinised. Wanting something is not a character flaw, but it is a predictable state, and attackers target states rather than people.

Remote work has widened this considerably. Ten years ago a fake job needed a fake office. Now the entire hiring process legitimately happens over email, a video call, and a document signed electronically, so an attacker does not have to fabricate anything a real employer would not also be doing. There is no physical mismatch to notice.

What makes these worth their own module is that the damage is not always what you expect. Losing a password is recoverable. The money mule variant, where you receive funds and forward them on for a percentage, ends with your bank account frozen and your name attached to a laundering investigation, regardless of whether you understood what you were doing. Intent is difficult to prove after the fact, and the transactions are in your name.

How the attack works

The sequence below is the pattern behind almost every version of this attack. Recognising the shape matters more than memorising any single example.

  1. Harvest candidates from public profiles. Anyone listing skills, availability, or an open-to-work status on a professional network is a target list, and the list is free.
  2. Borrow a real company's identity. An existing firm with a thin careers page is ideal, because the company checks out while the role is impossible to verify against a real listing.
  3. Open with flattery tied to something specific. Naming your actual portfolio piece proves only that they can read, but it feels like recognition, and recognition lowers scrutiny more reliably than money does.
  4. Offer slightly above market and a shortened process. Skipping a screening step is framed as a compliment. It is really a way to reach the payload before you have spoken to a second human being.
  5. Move off-platform quickly. Telegram or WhatsApp, because those channels are unmonitored, unreportable, and leave the professional network with no record to investigate.
  6. Deliver the payload. A portal that wants your email credentials, a fee for equipment or certification, onboarding paperwork demanding government ID and bank details, or a first task that involves receiving and forwarding money.
  7. Apply time pressure at the end. The role closes Friday, another candidate is waiting, the budget expires this quarter. Urgency arrives only once the request is on the table.

A real-world example

The money mule role, disguised as payment operations. This is a reconstruction built for teaching. No real person or company is involved.

Simulated attack, example only
From: Talent Team <careers@northbridge-digital.recruit-hub.co>
To: you@example.com
Subject: Remote Payment Operations Associate, ₱45,000/mo, flexible hours

Hi Juan,

We are expanding our Asia-Pacific operations and need a detail-oriented associate to help process client disbursements. Part-time, fully remote, around 8 to 10 hours a week.

The role: client funds are transferred into your personal account, you deduct your 7% handling commission, and forward the balance to the supplier accounts we provide. We are setting up our local corporate banking and this bridges the gap for the next quarter.

No interview required. We have reviewed your profile and are happy to proceed. Please confirm and send a photo of a government ID plus your bank details so we can begin on Monday.

Best,
Talent Team

What gives it away. This is money laundering with a job title. No legitimate company moves client funds through an employee's personal bank account, because no legitimate company can, and their own banking would never be described as a gap you personally bridge. The commission is deliberately generous enough to feel like a reason and small enough to feel plausible. Notice the domain too: the real-sounding northbridge-digital is only a subdomain of recruit-hub.co, which is who actually owns the address. And no interview for a role handling money is not a shortcut, it is the whole point, because a conversation is where this falls apart.

Red flags to look for

Each of these is something you can actually observe in the message or the request, not a vague feeling. One flag is reason to slow down. Two is reason to stop.

  • You are asked to receive money and forward it on, for any reason, at any commission. This is money laundering regardless of how it is described.
  • A portal or skills assessment requires you to sign in with your Google, Microsoft, or Apple account.
  • You are asked to pay for anything: training, certification, equipment, a background check, or a software licence. Legitimate employers never charge you to be hired.
  • Government ID, bank details, or a tax number are requested before any contract exists.
  • The conversation moves to Telegram or WhatsApp early, away from the platform where the approach began.
  • There is no interview, or a normal screening step is skipped because of how impressed they are.
  • The pay is noticeably above market for the hours described, and the hours are vague.
  • The domain looks right at a glance but the real owner is the part just before the first single slash, not the brand name in front of it.
  • The recruiter's profile is recent, has few connections, no posting history, and links to a company page with no other employees.

What to do if it happens to you

Work through these in order. The first hour matters far more than getting every step perfect.

  1. Search the company name plus the word scam before replying. Thirty seconds, and it resolves a surprising number of these.
  2. Find the company's real website yourself rather than through their link, and check whether the role is listed on the careers page. If it is not, that is your answer.
  3. Contact the company through their published number or address and ask whether the recruiter works there. Real employers answer this readily.
  4. Never enter email credentials for an assessment. There is no legitimate version of that request, so it needs no further analysis.
  5. If you already sent ID documents, keep a record of what was sent and when, watch for accounts opened in your name, and consider asking your bank to flag the account.
  6. If money has already passed through your account, stop immediately and contact your bank yourself. Explain that you believe you were recruited into moving funds. Going to them first is a materially better position than waiting for them to come to you.
  7. Report the profile and the message on the platform where it started, and to the PNP Anti-Cybercrime Group or NBI Cybercrime Division if money moved.

One rule covers almost every version: a real job costs you nothing and never asks for your credentials. Any offer that inverts either half is not an offer.

It is worth being kind to yourself about this one. These approaches are engineered to land when you are between contracts and hoping, and being taken in reflects timing rather than judgement. The people who lose the most are usually the ones who realise partway through and then feel too embarrassed to stop. Stopping late is still stopping, and it is always better than finishing.
