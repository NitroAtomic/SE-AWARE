Vishing

A scam phone call that uses live pressure to make you act before you have time to verify anything.

6 min read 10-question quiz at the end No account needed

What it is

Vishing is a scam conducted by voice: a phone call, or sometimes a voice note, where the attacker talks you into giving up a code, a password, or remote access to your computer. The name comes from “voice phishing”, and the goal is identical to email phishing. What changes is the medium, and the medium changes everything.

A phishing email waits for you. You can leave it unopened, read it twice, forward it to someone, or check the sender address at your own pace. A phone call does none of that. Someone is speaking to you right now, expecting an answer right now, and the social cost of being rude to a stranger who sounds professional is surprisingly effective at overriding caution. Skepticism that comes easily in writing is genuinely harder to hold onto out loud.

In the survey conducted for this project, 81.5% of the 54 remote workers had already received suspicious phone calls. The two dominant patterns are a fake bank fraud team calling about a transaction you did not make, and fake IT support calling about a problem with your account, a pretext that works particularly well on remote workers, who genuinely do receive help from IT people they have never met in person.

How the attack works

The sequence below is the pattern behind almost every version of this attack. Recognising the shape matters more than memorising any single example.

  1. Gather just enough detail. Your name, your employer, the bank you use, usually from a previous breach or from your own public profiles. A caller who opens with a fact about you buys instant credibility.
  2. Spoof the caller ID. Caller ID is trivially forged. A call can display your bank's real hotline number, so seeing a familiar number on screen proves nothing at all.
  3. Open with authority and alarm. “This is the fraud team. We've blocked a ₱48,000 transfer from your account. I need to verify your identity to release the hold.” Fear plus authority produces compliance faster than any other combination.
  4. Build credibility with facts you already know. The caller recites your full name and the last four digits of your card. These usually come from a breach, and they are offered specifically so that you will conclude the call is genuine.
  5. Keep you on the line. Verification is easy when you have time to think, so the caller creates continuous pressure and often explicitly asks you not to hang up.
  6. Make the real request. Read back the code we just sent you. Install this support tool so we can secure your device. Move your money to a safe holding account while we investigate.
  7. Use it immediately. The code is entered on the real site within seconds. The remote-access tool gives full control of your machine. The “safe account” belongs to the attacker.

A real-world example

A vishing call script. This is a reconstruction built for teaching. No real person or company is involved.

Simulated attack, example only
+63 2 8631 8000
Incoming call · matches a saved contact
“Good afternoon, am I speaking with Juan Paolo Dente? This is Reggie from the fraud monitoring department. I'm calling about an attempted transfer of ₱48,750 to an account in Cebu at 2:14 this afternoon. Did you authorise that?

No? Good, we've held it, but the hold expires in fifteen minutes and after that it processes automatically. I can cancel it permanently, I just need to verify I'm speaking to the account holder.

I'm sending a six-digit code to the mobile number ending in 4471 right now. Please read it back to me so I can confirm your identity and release the block. And please stay on the line. If we disconnect I can't guarantee the hold will hold.”
What gives it away. The caller ID displays the bank's genuine hotline, which is spoofed and means nothing. The script uses your real full name and a plausible card ending, both cheap to obtain. But the entire call collapses on one request: read the code back to me. The code was generated because the attacker was already logging in as you. No bank on earth needs you to read an OTP aloud. They sent it, so they know what it is. The instruction not to hang up exists solely to prevent you from verifying.

Red flags to look for

Each of these is something you can actually observe in the message or the call, not a vague feeling. One flag is reason to slow down. Two is reason to stop.

  • Anyone asks you to read out, type, or forward a one-time PIN, verification code, or authentication code. This is always an attack. There is no legitimate exception.
  • The caller creates urgency measured in minutes and discourages you from hanging up, calling back, or checking with anyone.
  • You are asked to install remote-access software (AnyDesk, TeamViewer, QuickSupport, or anything similar) so someone can “fix” or “secure” your device.
  • You are told to move money to a “safe”, “holding”, or “verification” account. No such thing exists in real banking.
  • The caller asks for your full password, your PIN, your full card number, or your card's CVV.
  • The caller ID matches a real institution but the request does not. Caller ID is display text, not proof of origin.
  • Payment is requested in gift cards, e-wallet transfers, or cryptocurrency, all chosen because they are irreversible.
  • Someone claims to be from IT support about a problem you never reported, and asks you to confirm credentials.
  • A familiar voice on a call or voice note makes an unusual and urgent request for money. AI voice cloning now needs only a short sample of public audio.

What to do if it happens to you

Work through these in order. The first hour matters far more than getting every step perfect.

  1. Hang up. This is not rude, and it costs you nothing if the call was genuine. Ending a call is always safe; staying on one is where the risk lives.
  2. Wait a moment, then call back on a number you already have, printed on your card, on your statement, or on the company's official website. Never a number the caller gave you, and never by redialling the incoming number.
  3. Use a different phone if you can. Some scams keep the line open so your “callback” reaches the same person. Waiting a minute or using another device removes that risk.
  4. If you read out a code, act immediately. Change that account's password from another device, revoke all active sessions, and call your bank on their official hotline if money is involved.
  5. If you installed remote-access software, disconnect from the internet first, then uninstall it, then change every important password from a different device. Assume anything typed on the compromised machine was seen.
  6. If money moved, call your bank within minutes. Recall is sometimes possible in the first hour and rarely possible after that. Ask them to file a fraud report and give you a reference number.
  7. Agree a code word with family and key clients. Against AI voice cloning, a shared word that has never been posted online is the simplest defence that actually works.

If you remember nothing else from this module, remember this: an OTP is a password that expires quickly. No bank, courier, employer, platform, or IT department will ever ask you to read one out. The moment a caller asks for a code, the call is over, not because you have been rude, but because you have just been told exactly what the call was for.
