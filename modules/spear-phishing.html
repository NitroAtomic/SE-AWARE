Spear Phishing

Phishing written specifically for you, using real details gathered from your public professional footprint.

6 min read 10-question quiz at the end No account needed

What it is

Spear phishing is phishing written for one specific person. Where ordinary phishing is a net thrown across millions of inboxes hoping a few people bite, spear phishing is a single message built after someone has spent real time reading about you. It names your actual client. It refers to the project you posted about last week. It arrives from a person whose job title makes the request sound completely ordinary.

Remote workers are unusually exposed to this, and the reason is structural rather than careless. Finding work online requires being findable. A freelancer's LinkedIn lists their skills, their rates, and their recent clients. A portfolio site names the companies they have worked with. A public post celebrates landing a new contract. Every one of those is reasonable professional behaviour, and every one of them is free reconnaissance.

The uncomfortable thing about spear phishing is that the usual advice stops working. “Watch for generic greetings” is useless when the message opens with your first name and mentions your actual deliverable. The detail that would normally reassure you is precisely what has been weaponised. So the defence has to shift: instead of asking does this message look legitimate, you ask can I confirm this person through a channel I already had before this message arrived.

How the attack works

The sequence below is the pattern behind almost every version of this attack. Recognising the shape matters more than memorising any single example.

  1. Pick the target. Often someone with access but without a security team behind them: a freelancer, a virtual assistant, a contractor, or a junior employee who handles invoices or scheduling.
  2. Research from public sources. LinkedIn gives the job history, the connections, and the current employer. A portfolio site gives client names. Social media gives the tone of voice, the timezone, and often the current project.
  3. Map the relationships. Who does this person report to? Who pays them? Which platform do they invoice through? Impersonating the right person matters more than writing the perfect email.
  4. Craft one message. Correct names, correct project, correct timing. Frequently sent on a Friday afternoon or just before a holiday, when verification is hardest and the pressure to finish is highest.
  5. Make a request that fits the story. Review the attached brief. Sign in to the client portal. Update the payment details for this month's invoice. Nothing dramatic. The request has to sound like a normal Tuesday.
  6. Sustain the conversation if needed. Unlike bulk phishing, a spear phisher will happily exchange several friendly messages first, because the payoff justifies the patience.

A real-world example

A fake recruiter approach on a professional network. This is a reconstruction built for teaching. No real person or company is involved.

Simulated attack, example only
MR
Maria Reyes
Senior Talent Partner · Northbridge Digital
Hi Juan! I came across your portfolio. The analytics dashboard you built for Cortez Logistics is exactly the kind of work our client is after.
We're hiring a remote data visualisation contractor, ₱95k/month, fully async, starting in two weeks. Given your background I'd like to fast-track you past the first screen.
Could you complete the short skills assessment on our portal? It takes about 15 minutes. You'll need to sign in with your Google account so the results attach to your profile: northbridge-careers.talentportal-hr.com
Let me know once it's done and I'll set up the call. 🙂
What gives it away. Everything factual in this message is true and publicly available: the portfolio, the client name, the skill. That is the point. The single manipulated element is the last one: a “skills assessment” that requires signing in with Google on a domain owned by talentportal-hr.com, not by Northbridge. A real assessment never needs your email credentials. Note also the flattery, the above-market rate, and the offer to skip a screening step: three ways of making you feel you have something to lose by being cautious.

Red flags to look for

Each of these is something you can actually observe in the message or the call, not a vague feeling. One flag is reason to slow down. Two is reason to stop.

  • The message is unusually well-informed about your work but arrives from someone you have never interacted with before.
  • You are asked to sign in with Google, Microsoft, or Apple on a domain that is not the company's own. A recruitment portal has no reason to want your email credentials.
  • The offer is noticeably better than market rate, or a normal step in the process is being skipped “because of your background”.
  • The sender's domain is close to a real company's but not identical: an extra hyphen, a swapped letter, .co instead of .com, or a plausible-sounding subdomain.
  • A supervisor, client, or accounts contact emails about payment from a slightly different address than the one in your existing thread. Check the full address, not the display name.
  • The request arrives late on a Friday, just before a holiday, or while the person it claims to be from is known to be travelling.
  • You are asked to keep the matter confidential, or discouraged from raising it with anyone else.
  • An attachment or link is framed as a brief, contract, NDA, or assessment and requires credentials to open.
  • The tone pushes urgency in a relationship that has never been urgent before.

What to do if it happens to you

Work through these in order. The first hour matters far more than getting every step perfect.

  1. Verify the person, not the message. Look the company up independently, find its real website, and contact the person through a number or address you found yourself, never one supplied in the message.
  2. Use a channel that predates the message. If it claims to be your client, reply in the thread you already have with them, or call the number in your existing contacts. Do not reply to the new email.
  3. Check whether the person exists at all. A real recruiter has a history: a profile with connections, activity, and a company page that matches. A profile created three weeks ago with forty connections is a strong signal.
  4. Never enter email credentials for a job assessment. There is no legitimate version of that request.
  5. Treat any change in payment details as a stop signal. Confirm it by voice with someone you already know, using a number you already had.
  6. If you already signed in on the fake page change that password immediately from another device, revoke all active sessions, turn on multi-factor authentication, and check for forwarding rules added to your mailbox.
  7. Warn the impersonated party. If someone is using your client's name to reach you, that client needs to know, and so do their other contractors.

The single rule that survives contact with a well-crafted spear phishing message is this: detail is not identity. Someone knowing your project, your client, and your rate proves only that they can read. Before you act on anything involving credentials, money, or access, confirm the person through a channel that existed before their message arrived.
