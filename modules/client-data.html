Secure Client Data Handling

You hold access that would need formal approval inside a company, usually with no security onboarding attached.

Premium 7 min read 10-question quiz at the end

What it is

Freelancers and virtual assistants routinely hold access that would require a formal approval process inside a company: client inboxes, customer databases, ad accounts, financial records, admin credentials. You get it because you need it to do the work, and almost always with no security onboarding attached. Nobody sits you down and explains what you are now responsible for.

That matters for two separate reasons. The first is that it makes you a target. An attacker who wants a company's customer list has a choice between the company, which has a security team and monitored logins, and the contractor, who has the same data on a laptop that also runs the household. You are the softer route to the same prize, which is not a comment on your competence but on your resources.

The second is accountability. When client data leaks through your machine, the conversation is about what you did to protect it, and good intentions are not evidence. Being able to describe how you stored it, how long you kept it, and who could reach it is the difference between an incident and a liability. This module is about being able to give that answer.

How the attack works

The sequence below is the pattern behind almost every version of this attack. Recognising the shape matters more than memorising any single example.

  1. Collect less than you are offered. Ask whether you need the full export or three fields, ongoing access or access for one task. Data you never held cannot leak from you, and this single habit removes more risk than every technical control combined.
  2. Ask for delegated access instead of shared logins. Your own user account on the client's platform can be revoked cleanly when the contract ends, leaves an audit trail that protects you as much as them, and survives their staff changes.
  3. Work inside the client's systems. Their Drive, their project tool, their CRM. Copying data into your personal accounts multiplies the places it can leak from and blurs who is responsible when it does.
  4. Separate the work from everything else. A different user account on your machine at minimum, a different browser profile at the absolute minimum, so a family member's download cannot reach a client's files.
  5. Encrypt the disk and lock the screen. BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on macOS, a short auto-lock timeout. A stolen unencrypted laptop hands over every client file on it, and laptops are stolen for the hardware.
  6. Never send credentials over chat or email. Use the client's own permission system, or a password manager's sharing feature. A password in a chat log outlives the conversation by years.
  7. Delete at the end of the engagement. Remove client files from your machine and your cloud storage unless you have been asked to retain them. Old archives are pure liability: no upside, permanent exposure.

A real-world example

The request that is reasonable and still wrong. This is a reconstruction built for teaching. No real person or company is involved.

Simulated attack, example only
AV
Anna Villaruel
Marketing Lead · a real client, on her real account
Hi! Could you pull the full customer export from the CRM and drop it in your Drive so we can both work off it this week? Our IT have locked external sharing again and it is driving me mad.
Just the whole table is fine, easier than filtering. Everything from 2023 onwards.
What gives it away is that nothing does. Anna is real, the account is genuine, and the request is sincere. This is not an attack, and that is exactly why it belongs here. A full customer export sitting in your personal Drive is now outside the client's controls, outside their retention policy, and inside an account they cannot audit or revoke. If your Drive is breached six months from now, the disclosure conversation is about your storage, not theirs. The right answer is not refusal, it is a smaller version of yes: ask which fields she actually needs, offer to work inside their system, and if it truly has to sit somewhere shared, ask her to create a folder on their Drive and add you to it. You will be right, and the request will still get done.

Red flags to look for

Each of these is something you can actually observe in the message or the request, not a vague feeling. One flag is reason to slow down. Two is reason to stop.

  • You are asked to copy client data into your own storage because the client's sharing controls are inconvenient.
  • A request is for everything rather than the fields the task needs.
  • Credentials are shared with you over chat or email rather than through a permission system.
  • You are given an owner-level or admin login instead of your own user account.
  • There is no stated end point: no retention period, no deletion step, no offboarding conversation.
  • A new contact at the client asks for an export, a client list, or an organisation chart early in the relationship.
  • You are still holding files from a project that closed months ago.
  • Client work happens on a device that other people in your household also use.
  • Nobody, including you, could say today exactly which client data is on your machine and where.

What to do if it happens to you

Work through these in order. The first hour matters far more than getting every step perfect.

  1. Tell the client the same day. Fast disclosure is forgiven and late discovery is not, and they may have regulatory obligations with clocks already running.
  2. Write down what was accessed, when, and by what route, while it is fresh. This becomes the timeline everyone asks for later.
  3. Revoke the access involved and rotate anything that was shared, including passwords you believe were unaffected.
  4. Disconnect the device if you installed anything or granted remote access, then change credentials from a different machine.
  5. Do not delete anything to tidy up. Preserving evidence matters more than looking organised, and deletion looks far worse in hindsight than the incident itself.
  6. If personal data belonging to other people was exposed, the client may have to notify the National Privacy Commission, which is another reason they need to hear it from you immediately rather than eventually.
  7. Afterwards, write down what you would change and actually change one thing. An incident that produces no change in practice will happen again.

Put a short clause in your contract covering how client data is stored, how long you keep it, and what happens at the end of the engagement. Two or three sentences is enough. It sets expectations before there is a disagreement, it gives you a reason to decline the full-export request without it feeling personal, and it makes you look like a professional who has thought about this, because by then you will have.

The underlying idea is simple: hold as little as possible, for as short a time as possible, in a place the client can see. Everything else in this module is a consequence of that sentence.
