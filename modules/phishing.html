Phishing

A fake message that impersonates a trusted organisation to steal your credentials, your money, or your access.

6 min read 10-question quiz at the end No account needed

What it is

Phishing is a message that pretends to come from an organisation you already trust, sent for one purpose: to make you hand over something valuable before you stop to think. That something is usually a password, a one-time PIN, a card number, or an approval that moves money. The message itself carries no virus and exploits no software bug. It works entirely on you.

The word covers a family of related attacks that share the same shape but arrive by different routes. Email phishing is the classic version: a message that appears to come from your bank, a courier, a marketplace, or a platform you use for work. Fake login pages are the second half of that attack, the link in the email leads to a page that is a pixel-accurate copy of the real sign-in screen, and anything you type into it goes straight to the attacker. QR phishing, sometimes called quishing, replaces the visible link with a square of pixels, which is a particularly effective trick because a QR code gives you nothing to inspect before you scan it.

What makes phishing worth understanding first is that it is the foundation. Spear phishing is phishing with research behind it. Smishing is phishing delivered by text. Vishing is phishing conducted by voice. Learn to read one phishing email properly and you have most of what you need for everything else on this site.

How the attack works

The sequence below is the pattern behind almost every version of this attack. Recognising the shape matters more than memorising any single example.

  1. Choose a brand worth impersonating. The attacker picks an organisation you are likely to deal with and likely to obey: a bank, a courier, a government agency, a payment platform, or a workplace tool like Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace.
  2. Build the message. Real logos are copied from the company's own website. The layout is lifted from a genuine notification. Machine translation and large language models have removed the clumsy grammar that used to give these away, so bad English is no longer a reliable signal.
  3. Manufacture a reason to hurry. Your account will be suspended. A payment failed. A package is held at customs. Someone signed in from another country. The emotion varies but the function is identical: compress the time between reading and clicking.
  4. Send at scale. Ordinary phishing is a numbers game. Millions of messages go out, and a response rate of a fraction of a percent is a profitable campaign.
  5. Capture the credentials. The link opens a copy of the real login page, often on a domain that reads correctly at a glance. You type your username and password. The page captures both.
  6. Defeat the second factor in real time. Modern phishing kits do not stop at the password. The fake page immediately asks for your one-time PIN and relays it to the real site while the code is still valid, so the attacker signs in as you within seconds.
  7. Use the access. Sometimes immediately: transfer funds, redirect a payout. Sometimes patiently: read your mail for weeks to learn who pays you and when, then send an invoice with new bank details.

A real-world example

A phishing email aimed at a freelancer. This is a reconstruction built for teaching. No real person or company is involved.

Simulated attack, example only
From: PayPal Service <service@paypal-secure-verify.com>
To: you@example.com
Subject: Action required: your account will be limited in 24 hours

We noticed unusual activity on your account.

A login attempt from Lagos, Nigeria was blocked on 14 March at 03:12. For your protection we have placed a temporary limitation on your account.

You must confirm your identity within 24 hours or your account will be permanently restricted and any pending payouts will be reversed.

Verify My Account Now

PayPal will never ask for your password by email.

What gives it away. The sender domain is paypal-secure-verify.com, not paypal.com. Everything before the final dot is attacker-controlled, so a real brand name in the middle of a domain means nothing. The greeting is generic where a real service would use your name. The deadline is artificial. And note the last line: quoting the company's real security policy is a common trick to borrow credibility. The fastest response is not to examine the email harder, but to ignore it entirely and open the PayPal app you already have.

Red flags to look for

Each of these is something you can actually observe in the message or the call, not a vague feeling. One flag is reason to slow down. Two is reason to stop.

  • The domain after the final dot is not the company's real one. paypal.com.secure-login.net belongs to secure-login.net, not to PayPal. Read domains from the right.
  • The reply-to address does not match the sender's display name. The display name is free text; anyone can set it to anything.
  • You are addressed as “Dear Customer” or “Dear User” by a company that has always used your actual name.
  • There is a deadline measured in hours, paired with a consequence you would not want: suspension, reversal, a lost payment.
  • A link's visible text does not match where it actually goes. Hover on desktop, long-press on mobile, and read the real address before you tap.
  • You are asked to log in from a link rather than from the app or bookmark you normally use.
  • An attachment arrives unexpectedly, especially .html .htm .zip .iso, or a document that demands you enable macros.
  • The message asks for a one-time PIN, a password, or a full card number. No legitimate organisation ever asks for these by email.
  • Something is slightly off in the branding: an old logo, a stretched image, inconsistent fonts, a footer address that no longer exists.

What to do if it happens to you

Work through these in order. The first hour matters far more than getting every step perfect.

  1. Stop before you type anything. If you have only opened the message, you are fine. Reading a phishing email does not compromise you; responding to it does.
  2. Do not use any link, number, or address in the message, including the one in the footer that looks official. Open the app you already have installed, or type the address you already know.
  3. If you entered a password, change it now, from a different device. Change it anywhere else you reused it too. Then look for “sign out of all sessions” or “active devices” in the account's security settings and revoke everything.
  4. If you entered a one-time PIN, treat the account as already compromised. The attacker most likely used it within seconds. Change the password, revoke sessions, and check for forwarding rules or new recovery addresses that were added without your knowledge.
  5. Check your email rules specifically. A common follow-up move is to add a silent forwarding rule so the attacker keeps reading your mail long after you have changed the password.
  6. Tell your client or employer the same day. An early report is inconvenient. A late one is a much bigger problem for everyone.
  7. Report it, then delete it. Use your mail client's report-phishing button, forward it to your organisation's security contact, and in the Philippines you can report to the PNP Anti-Cybercrime Group or the NBI Cybercrime Division.

One habit is worth more than every checklist above: never sign in from a link you did not go looking for. If a message says your account needs attention, close it, open the app or the bookmark you already trust, and see whether the same notice is waiting for you there. If it is real, it will be. If it is not, you have lost ten seconds.
