Pretexting

A believable cover story built patiently over several messages before any request is ever made.

6 min read 10-question quiz at the end No account needed

What it is

Pretexting is the construction of a believable story, or pretext, that makes a later request seem entirely ordinary. Where phishing fires a single message and hopes, pretexting invests. The attacker builds an identity, establishes a reason to be in contact, exchanges a few unremarkable messages, and only then asks for the thing they came for. By that point the request does not feel like an attack. It feels like Thursday.

This is the slow, expensive end of social engineering, and it is where the largest losses happen. Business email compromise, the family of attacks in which someone impersonates a supplier, an executive, or a contractor to redirect a payment, is consistently among the costliest categories of cybercrime worldwide, and pretexting is the technique underneath it.

For remote workers the exposure is specific and predictable. You already conduct entire professional relationships with people you have never met, over email and chat, with clients whose voices you would not recognise. A convincing stranger claiming to be a new project manager at a client company is not suspicious in that environment. It is Tuesday.

How the attack works

The sequence below is the pattern behind almost every version of this attack. Recognising the shape matters more than memorising any single example.

  1. Research the relationship. Who do you work with, who pays you, who has authority over you, and what does a normal request from them look like? Much of this is visible in public posts and company pages.
  2. Build the identity. A domain registered a week ago, one letter different from the real one. A professional profile with a plausible photo. A signature block copied from a genuine employee.
  3. Establish contact without asking for anything. A friendly introduction. A question about a deliverable. A document to review. The first contact is deliberately harmless, and its only job is to make the second contact unremarkable.
  4. Let time pass. A gap of days between introduction and request makes the exchange feel like an established relationship rather than an approach.
  5. Make the request inside the story. The bank details on this month's invoice have changed. Please onboard this new vendor. The client needs the shared credentials before tomorrow's call.
  6. Apply just enough pressure. Not panic. That breaks the illusion. A soft deadline: the finance cut-off is Friday, the director is travelling and cannot be reached, it would be good to get this closed today.
  7. Discourage verification without seeming to. “I'm in meetings all day, email is best.” “Please keep this between us until the announcement.” Both make a phone call feel awkward rather than forbidden.

A real-world example

Invoice fraud through a pretexted vendor change. This is a reconstruction built for teaching. No real person or company is involved.

Simulated attack, example only
From: Anna Villaruel <a.villaruel@cortez-Iogistics.com>
To: you@example.com
Subject: Re: March retainer (updated remittance details)

Hi Juan

Thanks for turning the dashboard revisions around so quickly last week. Marco was really happy with it.

Quick admin note before Friday's finance cut-off: we've moved our disbursements to a new banking partner this quarter, so please update the account on your March invoice to the details below. The old account is being closed on the 28th and anything sent there will bounce back and delay you by another cycle.

Account name: Cortez Logistics Services Inc.
Bank: Union Bank of the Philippines
Account no.: 0074 1129 8830

Sorry for the hassle. I'm in workshops most of today but email reaches me fine.

Best,
Anna
Anna Villaruel · Finance Operations · Cortez Logistics

What gives it away. Look closely at the domain: cortez-Iogistics.com uses a capital I where a lowercase l belongs. In most fonts those are visually identical, and this is one of the most reliable tricks in the entire field. Everything else is real: the project, the colleague's name, the friendly opener, all harvested from a genuine earlier exchange. Notice the three pressure points working together: a deadline, a penalty for missing it, and a polite explanation of why a phone call is inconvenient. The bank change is the whole attack; everything else is scaffolding.

Red flags to look for

Each of these is something you can actually observe in the message or the call, not a vague feeling. One flag is reason to slow down. Two is reason to stop.

  • Bank details, a payee name, or a remittance address changes, especially with an explanation for why calling is inconvenient right now.
  • The domain is one character different from the real one: rn for m, capital I for lowercase l, a zero for the letter O, an added or dropped hyphen.
  • The email address is different from your existing thread with that person, even though the display name and signature are identical.
  • The request arrives just before a finance deadline, a holiday, or a period when the real person is known to be unavailable.
  • You are asked to keep the matter quiet, handle it directly, or skip a normal approval step.
  • The sender declines calls or video for a reason that sounds reasonable: travelling, in workshops, poor signal, phone being replaced.
  • A new contact at a familiar client introduces themselves and, within a few messages, needs access, credentials, or a payment change.
  • Someone requests a copy of an invoice, a client list, or an organisation chart, useful reconnaissance dressed up as admin.
  • A first request is small and harmless, and the second one is not. Escalation across messages is the signature of a pretext.

What to do if it happens to you

Work through these in order. The first hour matters far more than getting every step perfect.

  1. Treat every change of payment details as a hard stop. No exceptions, no matter how well you know the sender. This one rule prevents most invoice fraud on its own.
  2. Verify by voice, on a number you already had. Not the number in the signature of the new email, the one in your contacts, from before this conversation started. Say the account number out loud and have them confirm it.
  3. Compare the full email address against your existing thread, character by character. Do not trust the display name, which is free text.
  4. Reply inside the original thread rather than starting a new message. If the address was spoofed, your reply goes to the real person and the attack surfaces immediately.
  5. Slow the request down deliberately. A genuine colleague will accept a twenty-minute delay for verification. Only an attacker cannot afford one.
  6. If you already paid, call your bank within minutes and ask for a recall, then report it to the client whose identity was used. Other contractors are being targeted in the same campaign.
  7. If you shared credentials or documents, change what can be changed and tell the affected party the same day so they can watch for follow-on attacks.

Pretexting is defeated by procedure rather than by suspicion, and that is good news. Procedure works even on a bad day. Adopt one rule and hold it without exception: any change to where money goes gets confirmed by voice, on a number you already had, before anything is paid. It costs a two-minute call. Not having it has cost people their entire quarter.
