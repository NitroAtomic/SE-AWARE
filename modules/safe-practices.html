Safe Practices for Remote Workers

The everyday habits that make every attack on this platform harder to pull off against you. Work down the list in order. It is arranged by how much protection each habit buys for the effort it costs.

8 min read Reference guide, no quiz No account needed
If you only do two things turn on multi-factor authentication for your email account and start using a password manager. Between them those two habits neutralise the majority of what a stolen password can be used for.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA, sometimes called two-factor authentication or 2FA) means a stolen password on its own is not enough to get into your account. After the password, the service asks for a second thing: a code from an app, a tap on your phone, or a physical key.

Start with your email account, not your bank. This surprises people, but your email is the master key to everything else you own, every “forgot password” link on every other service lands there. An attacker who controls your email can reset your bank, your marketplace accounts, and your client portals at leisure. Secure it first, then work outward.

Not all second factors are equal. From weakest to strongest:

  • SMS codes, far better than nothing, but vulnerable to SIM-swap fraud and to the vishing calls covered in this platform's Vishing module.
  • Authenticator apps (Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, Authy). Codes are generated on your device and never travel over the network. This is the right default for most people.
  • Hardware security keys (YubiKey and similar), the strongest option, and effectively immune to phishing because the key checks the website's real address before it responds.
Before you finish setup, save your backup codes offline. Print them, or write them down and keep them somewhere physical. Losing your phone without backup codes can lock you out of your own accounts permanently.

The single most damaging password habit is not weakness. It is reuse. When any site you have an account with is breached, attackers take the leaked email-and-password pairs and try them automatically across hundreds of other services. This is called credential stuffing, and it is why one forgotten forum account from 2016 can cost you your email today.

Length beats complexity. correct-horse-battery-staple is far stronger and far easier to remember than P@ssw0rd1, which follows a substitution pattern every cracking tool already knows. Aim for at least 16 characters and prefer a passphrase of several unrelated words.

Nobody can remember a hundred unique passphrases, which is exactly the problem a password manager solves. Bitwarden, 1Password, and KeePassXC all generate, store, and fill unique credentials for every site. You memorise one strong master passphrase and the manager handles the rest.

A password manager also quietly defends you against phishing: it fills credentials based on the site's real domain, so if you land on a convincing fake, it simply will not autofill. That silence is a warning worth listening to.

Check your exposure. Free breach-notification services such as Have I Been Pwned let you see whether an address of yours has appeared in a known breach. If it has, change that password and anywhere you reused it.

This is the habit that defeats the most expensive attacks on this platform, and it fits in one sentence: if a request involves money, credentials, or access, confirm it through a different channel than the one it arrived on.

An email asking you to change bank details gets confirmed by phone. A chat message from a client asking for credentials gets confirmed by email in the thread you already had. A call asking you to approve something gets confirmed by hanging up and calling back on a number you already had.

The word already is doing the real work. Use contact details you had before the request arrived, from your contacts list, an old invoice, or the company's official website. Never the number in the new email's signature, and never the number the caller reads out to you.

  • Any change to bank details, payee names, or remittance addresses, without exception, no matter how well you know the sender
  • Any request for a password, PIN, one-time code, or account access
  • Any urgent payment, especially one framed as confidential or as a favour
  • Any request to install software so someone can help you remotely

A genuine colleague or client will never object to a two-minute verification call. Only an attacker cannot afford one. If someone becomes irritated that you are checking, that reaction is itself the answer.

Working from home means your router is now part of your professional security, and most home routers ship in a state nobody ever revisits.

  • Change the router's admin password. Not the Wi-Fi password, the separate one that protects the router's settings page. Factory defaults like admin/admin are published online for every model.
  • Use WPA3 if your router supports it, WPA2 otherwise. If your network is still on WEP or has no password, fix that today.
  • Install firmware updates. Many routers can do this automatically; if yours cannot, check manually every few months.
  • Turn off WPS and remote administration unless you genuinely use them. Both are convenience features with a poor security history.
  • Put smart devices on a guest network. Smart TVs, cameras, and plugs are rarely well maintained. Keeping them off the network your work laptop uses limits the damage if one is compromised.

On public Wi-Fi (cafés, airports, coworking spaces), assume the network is hostile. Use a reputable VPN or your phone's mobile hotspot for anything involving client data, and never dismiss a browser certificate warning to get online faster.

The cleanest version of this is a device used only for work. That is not always affordable, so here is the practical ladder, from best to still-worthwhile:

  1. A separate physical device for client work. Ideal, and worth budgeting for if your income depends on it.
  2. A separate user account on the same machine. Free, takes ten minutes, and keeps files, browser profiles, and saved logins genuinely separated.
  3. At minimum, a separate browser profile for work, with its own extensions and saved sessions.

Whichever level you reach, keep two rules. First, nobody else uses your work profile, a family member installing a game or clicking a link is a real and common route to compromise. Second, keep client data off shared computers entirely, including the family desktop.

Also: enable full-disk encryption (BitLocker on Windows, FileVault on macOS), set a short auto-lock timeout, and turn on remote-wipe if your device supports it. A stolen unencrypted laptop hands over every client file on it.

As a freelancer or virtual assistant you are often trusted with more than your job title suggests, inboxes, customer lists, financial records, and admin credentials. That trust makes you a target, and it also makes you accountable.

  • Collect and keep only what the work requires. Data you never held cannot leak from you.
  • Delete client files when a project ends, unless you have been asked to retain them. Old archives are pure liability.
  • Never send credentials over chat or email. Ask the client to share access through their platform's own permission system, or use a password manager's secure sharing feature.
  • Ask for delegated access rather than shared logins. Being added as a user on their account is safer for both of you than being handed the owner's password.
  • Use the client's own tools where possible (their Drive, their project system) rather than copying data into your personal accounts.
  • Report incidents immediately. Clients forgive fast disclosure far more readily than they forgive discovering it themselves weeks later.

Updates are unglamorous and they matter. A large share of successful intrusions exploit vulnerabilities that were patched months or years earlier: the fix existed and simply was not installed.

  • Turn on automatic updates for your operating system and restart when asked, rather than postponing for weeks.
  • Keep your browser current. It is the single most exposed piece of software you run.
  • Audit browser extensions periodically and remove anything you no longer use. Extensions can read everything on the pages you visit, and popular ones are sometimes sold to new owners with different intentions.
  • Update your phone too. It holds your authenticator app and your one-time codes.
  • Install software only from official app stores or the vendor's real website. “Cracked” and repackaged software is a reliable delivery route for malware.

Decide where you would report something before you need to. Under pressure, having to research this costs the hour that matters most.

If you think you have just been caught, in this order:

  1. Disconnect the device from the internet if you installed anything or granted remote access.
  2. Change the affected password from a different device, then revoke all active sessions in that account's security settings.
  3. Call your bank immediately if money is involved. Recall is sometimes possible within the first hour and rarely after. Ask for a fraud reference number.
  4. Tell your client or employer the same day. Early beats tidy, every time.
  5. Preserve evidence: screenshots, full email headers, phone numbers, transaction references. Do not delete the message until it has been reported.

Where to report in the Philippines:

  • PNP Anti-Cybercrime Group, for cybercrime complaints, including online fraud
  • NBI Cybercrime Division, for investigation of cyber-related offences
  • Your bank's official fraud hotline, the number printed on your card or statement
  • The National Privacy Commission, if personal data belonging to others was exposed
  • The impersonated platform, Google, Meta, and most marketplaces have dedicated phishing report channels

Finally, tell the people around you. Scam campaigns run in waves through the same networks, and a short warning to your family, your colleagues, and your client's other contractors is one of the most effective things you can do.

A note on perfection. No one does all of this at once, and treating security as all-or-nothing is how people end up doing none of it. Pick the first item you have not done, do it this week, then come back for the next one.
