Invoice and Payment Scams

The attack that turns social engineering directly into lost income, in both directions: your invoices and your payments.

Premium 7 min read 10-question quiz at the end

What it is

Invoice fraud is where social engineering turns directly into lost income. Everything else on this platform costs you an account, a password, or an afternoon. This one costs you the money you have already earned, and it usually takes weeks to notice.

Freelancers are exposed from two directions at once, which is unusual. You send invoices, so your payments can be intercepted. You also pay people: subcontractors, tool subscriptions, occasionally a supplier on a client's behalf. Both directions are attacked with the same technique, and the money moves out of reach faster than a bank can be persuaded to look at it.

The reason it works is that a payment instruction is one of the few messages nobody questions. An email asking for your password sets off alarms. An email saying please use these updated bank details sets off nothing, because banks really do change, businesses really do restructure, and querying it feels like accusing a client of something. Attackers rely on that social discomfort just as much as on the technical spoof.

How the attack works

The sequence below is the pattern behind almost every version of this attack. Recognising the shape matters more than memorising any single example.

  1. Get inside a mailbox, or beside it. Either your account or the client's is compromised, usually through the phishing covered in the free modules, or a lookalike domain is registered so no compromise is needed at all.
  2. Read quietly and learn the rhythm. Who pays whom, on what day of the month, in what tone, with which reference numbers. This stage produces nothing visible and can run for weeks.
  3. Wait for a real invoice. The attack is timed to a genuine transaction, because a payment that was already expected raises no questions at all.
  4. Send the correction. A follow-up apologising for the confusion, explaining that the account on the invoice is being closed, and supplying new details. Often from a domain one character different, sometimes from the real mailbox itself.
  5. Add a soft deadline. The finance cut-off is Friday. The old account closes on the 28th. Anything sent there will bounce and delay you another cycle.
  6. Discourage a phone call without forbidding one. In workshops all day, email is best. The point is to make calling feel like a nuisance rather than a rule.
  7. Move the money onward within minutes. The receiving account is usually a mule account that is emptied immediately, which is why recall is only realistic in the first hour.

A real-world example

The overpayment refund, a variant that catches freelancers specifically. This is a reconstruction built for teaching. No real person or company is involved.

Simulated attack, example only
From: Daniel Ortega <d.ortega@brightpathmedia.co>
To: you@example.com
Subject: Payment sent, sorry about the mix-up

Hi Juan,

Payment for the branding work has gone out today. Our accounts assistant has made a mess of it though, she has processed the full retainer amount instead of the 50% deposit we agreed.

You should see ₱62,000 land rather than ₱31,000. Apologies. Could you send the ₱31,000 difference back to the account below? Our finance system will not let us reverse a completed transfer at our end, and I would rather sort it out this week than let it drag into the next reconciliation.

Account name: BrightPath Media Services
Bank: BPI
Account no.: 4471 0092 3318

Thanks for being flexible,
Daniel

What gives it away. The ₱62,000 is real and it really does land, which is what makes this version so effective. It was paid with a stolen card or a bad cheque, and it will be reversed by the bank days or weeks later. The ₱31,000 you send back is your own money, and it is gone the moment it leaves. Two details do the persuading: an apology, which puts you in the position of doing someone a favour, and the claim that their system cannot reverse it, which is false for every real bank. The correct answer is that a genuine overpayment is reversed by the sender's bank, never refunded by the recipient.

Red flags to look for

Each of these is something you can actually observe in the message or the request, not a vague feeling. One flag is reason to slow down. Two is reason to stop.

  • Bank details, a payee name, or a remittance address changes, for any reason, on any invoice.
  • An overpayment arrives and you are asked to return the difference. Treat this as fraud until the original payment has fully cleared, which can take weeks.
  • The correction arrives as a follow-up to a genuine invoice, referencing real amounts and real reference numbers.
  • The domain differs by one character: rn for m, a capital I for a lowercase l, a zero for the letter O, an added or dropped hyphen.
  • The message says their finance system cannot reverse or amend the transaction at their end. Real banks can always reverse their own outbound payment.
  • There is a deadline tied to a cut-off, a closure, or a reconciliation, paired with a penalty for missing it.
  • A reason is offered in advance for why a phone call is inconvenient.
  • You are asked to pay a new supplier, a subcontractor, or a fee on the client's behalf, to be reimbursed later.
  • Payment is requested in gift cards, e-wallet transfers, or cryptocurrency, all chosen because they cannot be reversed.

What to do if it happens to you

Work through these in order. The first hour matters far more than getting every step perfect.

  1. Stop before paying anything. No payment is so urgent that it cannot survive a two-minute call. That sentence is the entire module.
  2. Call on a number you already had, from your contacts or an old invoice, never the number in the signature of the new email. Read the account number aloud and have them confirm it digit by digit.
  3. Reply inside the original thread rather than starting a new message. If the address was spoofed, your reply reaches the real person and the impersonation surfaces immediately.
  4. For an overpayment, refuse to refund it yourself. Ask the sender to have their bank reverse the original transfer. A genuine payer can always do this. An attacker cannot.
  5. If you have already paid, call your bank within minutes and use the words authorised push payment fraud. Ask them to attempt a recall and to give you a fraud reference number.
  6. Then tell the client whose identity was used. Their other contractors are being targeted in the same campaign, and they may be able to warn people before more money moves.
  7. Preserve everything: the full email headers, the account details, timestamps, and any chat logs. Delete nothing until it has been reported.

Adopt one rule and hold it without exception: any change to where money goes gets confirmed by voice, on a number you already had, before anything is paid. Not for people you have just met, for everyone, including the client you have worked with for three years, because that is precisely the account worth compromising.

Two habits make you a harder target as well. Put a line on every invoice you send saying your bank details never change and that any email claiming otherwise should be verified by phone, which protects your clients and your own payments at once. And check your sent folder and mail rules every so often for anything you did not create, because a silent forwarding rule is how the attacker read the invoice in the first place.
