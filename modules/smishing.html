Smishing

Phishing delivered by SMS, where a short message and a shortened link do the work of a whole fake website.

5 min read 10-question quiz at the end No account needed

What it is

Smishing is phishing delivered by SMS. The name is a compression of “SMS” and “phishing”, and the mechanics are the same as email phishing with one important difference: text messages arrive on a device you check constantly, in a place you associate with people you actually know, and with almost none of the context that would help you evaluate them.

That last part matters more than it sounds. An email client shows you a full sender address, a subject line, a signature block, and a link you can hover over. A text message shows you a phone number or a sender ID that anyone can spoof, under 160 characters, with a shortened link that reveals nothing about where it goes. You are asked to make the same decision with a fraction of the evidence, usually while doing something else.

Among the 54 remote workers surveyed for this project, 92.6% had already received a prize or raffle scam text, the highest figure for any attack type in the study. Smishing is not an emerging threat for Filipino remote workers. It is a daily one.

How the attack works

The sequence below is the pattern behind almost every version of this attack. Recognising the shape matters more than memorising any single example.

  1. Acquire the numbers. From breached databases, from public listings, or simply by dialling ranges. Numbers are cheap and no targeting is required.
  2. Spoof the sender. Alphanumeric sender IDs like “BDO” or “LBC” can be forged, and on many phones a spoofed message will thread itself into the same conversation as genuine ones from that brand, which is why sitting under real messages proves nothing.
  3. Use one of three lures. A package needing a small fee, a prize you never entered for, or an urgent bank alert about a transaction you do not recognise. Between them these cover the overwhelming majority of scam texts.
  4. Shorten the link. A shortener hides the destination completely, and on a small screen even an unshortened address is easy to misread.
  5. Harvest on a mobile-optimised fake page. The landing page is built for phones and looks more convincing there than the same page would on a desktop.
  6. Take the small payment, or the credentials, or both. A ₱50 “redelivery fee” is low enough that many people pay without much thought, and now the attacker has full card details.

A real-world example

A package delivery smishing message. This is a reconstruction built for teaching. No real person or company is involved.

Simulated attack, example only
Today 9:41 AM · from +63 917 555 0148
LBC EXPRESS: Your parcel LB4471982PH is on hold at our sorting facility due to an incomplete address. Update your details and pay the ₱48 redelivery fee within 12 hours or the item will be returned to sender.

lbc-redelivery.info/ph4471
9:41 AM
What gives it away. The fee is deliberately small: ₱48 is below the threshold where most people stop to think, and the real prize is the card details you enter to pay it. The tracking number looks authentic but is invented; you have no way to check it except through the link, which is the whole design. The domain lbc-redelivery.info is not lbcexpress.com. And there is the twelve-hour deadline again. If you are genuinely expecting a parcel, open the courier's own app or type their real website yourself and paste the tracking number there.

Red flags to look for

Each of these is something you can actually observe in the message or the call, not a vague feeling. One flag is reason to slow down. Two is reason to stop.

  • A link is present in a message you did not expect. This alone is enough reason to stop. Almost no legitimate service needs you to open a link from an unsolicited text.
  • A small fee is requested to release, redeliver, or clear something. The amount is kept low on purpose.
  • You have won a prize, a raffle, or a cash reward in something you never entered.
  • The message uses a link shortener, or a domain that resembles a real brand with extra words or hyphens attached.
  • A deadline is measured in hours, and something will supposedly be lost if you miss it.
  • The sender is a mobile number rather than the brand's usual sender ID, or is a brand ID for a company you have no account with.
  • A bank alert arrives about a transaction you do not recognise, and the message conveniently supplies a number to call. That number belongs to the attacker.
  • A job offer arrives by text with high pay, no interview, and a request to message a Telegram or WhatsApp account.
  • Anyone at all asks you to forward a verification code you just received. This is always an attack, with no exceptions.

What to do if it happens to you

Work through these in order. The first hour matters far more than getting every step perfect.

  1. Do not tap the link. If you have already tapped but entered nothing, close the page. You are almost certainly fine.
  2. Verify through the app you already have. Open the courier's or bank's official app, or type their website address yourself. Every real notification will also be visible there.
  3. Never call a number supplied inside the message. Use the number printed on the back of your card, on your statement, or on the company's official site.
  4. If you entered card details, call your bank immediately using their official hotline. Ask them to block the card and check for pending transactions. Speed matters: a fresh card number gets sold and used within hours.
  5. If you entered a password or OTP, change that password now from a different device, revoke active sessions, and enable multi-factor authentication if it was not already on.
  6. Report and block. Forward the message to your network provider's spam reporting number, block the sender, then delete it. Do not reply, even to say no. A reply confirms your number is active and worth reselling.
  7. Tell the people around you. Smishing campaigns run in waves across a whole contact network. A five-second warning to family and colleagues is genuinely effective.

The habit to build is small and completely reliable: never act inside a text message. Read it, then close it, then go to the app or website you already trust and check there. Every legitimate notification you will ever receive is also waiting for you on the other side of that check.
